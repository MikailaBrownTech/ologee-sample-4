Restorative Practice in Healthcare Settings
4:22 / 12:08
🔊 ⛶

Overview

Healthcare environments present unique challenges for restorative practice — high-stakes decisions, power differentials between providers and patients, and systemic trauma that predates any individual interaction. This lesson examines how restorative principles can be applied in clinical, administrative, and community health contexts.

Key concept: Restorative practice in healthcare isn't about adjudicating harm after the fact — it's a proactive framework for building the relational infrastructure that makes harm less likely and recovery more possible.

The Three Entry Points

Practitioners typically enter restorative work in healthcare through one of three contexts:

  • Patient-provider conflict — when harm, real or perceived, has occurred during the care relationship
  • Staff-to-staff conflict — workplace tensions that affect team cohesion and patient outcomes
  • Community re-entry — supporting patients discharged from institutional care back into community settings
[ Diagram: Three Entry Points Model ]

Adapting Circle Practice

The traditional talking circle must be adapted for clinical environments. Key modifications include shorter session formats compatible with shift-based scheduling, attention to power dynamics between licensed providers and non-clinical staff, and integration with existing case management and social work structures.

How do you handle a community member who actively resists the restorative circle process — especially when their buy-in is needed for any agreement to hold?
AC
Dr. Amara Cole Instructor · 1h ago
Great question, Janelle. I usually start by having a private "pre-circle" conversation with the resistant participant — not to convince them, but to understand what's driving the resistance. Often it's fear of exposure, not unwillingness to repair. That changes the approach entirely.
RM
Rosalyn M. · 45m ago
This came up for me in a school context. We found that offering a "partial circle" where the resistant member could contribute a written statement instead of speaking in person reduced the resistance significantly.

Terrence W. · Yesterday · Chapter 2
In the "Role of the Keeper" reading, Dr. Cole mentions that the keeper should be emotionally neutral but not emotionally absent. Can someone unpack what that distinction looks like in practice?
AC
Dr. Amara Cole Instructor · Yesterday
Neutral means you don't take sides or push toward a particular outcome. Emotionally present means you're tracking the room's energy and responding — if grief surfaces, you honor it; if tension spikes, you slow down. The keeper is a witness, not a wall.
